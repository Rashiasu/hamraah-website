Your F-1 interview usually lasts two to five minutes. In that time a consular officer at the US Embassy in Islamabad or the Consulate in Karachi has to decide three things: are you a genuine student, can you pay for your studies, and do you plan to come back home after your degree?

Almost every question you hear is one of those three questions in different words. Once you understand that, the interview stops feeling like a test you can fail and starts feeling like a short conversation about your own plan.

This guide lists the questions Pakistani students are asked most often, explains what the officer is really checking, and shows how to answer. It is not a script. Officers hear memorised answers all day, and they can tell.

## Before you go: what has changed in 2025 and 2026

- **Social media must be public.** Since June 2025, the US consulates in Pakistan have asked F, M and J applicants to set their social media accounts to public so officers can review them, and the review was widened again from March 2026. List every account you have used in the last five years on your DS-160. Leaving one out can lead to a refusal.
- **Interview in Pakistan.** Since September 2025, applicants are expected to apply in their country of nationality or residence, so book your appointment in Pakistan rather than in a nearby country.
- **Fees.** The visa application (MRV) fee is $185 and the SEVIS I-901 fee is $350. A new $250 visa integrity fee, created by US law in 2025, is charged when a visa is issued, and consulates have been rolling it out at different speeds. Check the fees on the official appointment website before you pay.

!!! Good to know
Rules and fees change often. We check this article regularly, but the official sources are the US Embassy in Pakistan website and the appointment portal at ustraveldocs.com.

## Questions about your study plans

These check that you are a real student with a clear reason for this program.

### "Why did you choose this university?"

Give two or three specific reasons: a professor or research group, a course you cannot find in Pakistan, the program's structure, or a scholarship it offered you. "It has a good ranking" is weak. "Its Master's in Data Science has a required industry project, and Professor X's lab works on healthcare data, which is what I did at NUST" is strong.

### "Why this subject?" and "Why not study in Pakistan?"

Connect your past study or work to the program, and the program to the job you want afterwards. Be fair to Pakistan. You do not need to say Pakistani universities are bad. Explain what this program gives you that you cannot get at home.

### "Which other universities did you apply to?" and "Which ones admitted you?"

Answer honestly, including rejections. A sensible list that includes some ambitious and some safe choices shows planning.

### "What was your CGPA?" or "What was your GRE, IELTS or Duolingo score?"

Know your numbers exactly. If a grade was low, do not hide it. Say briefly what happened and what improved afterwards.

## Questions about money

The officer needs to believe your family can pay the full first-year cost on your I-20, and that the money is real and has a clear history.

### "Who is sponsoring you?"

Name your sponsor, their relationship to you, and their work. For example: "My father. He owns a textile wholesale business in Faisalabad and my mother is a government school teacher."

### "What does your sponsor do, and how much do they earn?"

Know the annual income in rupees and roughly in dollars. It should match your documents. If income comes from several sources, such as business, rent from property or agricultural land, say so simply.

### "How will you pay for the second, third and fourth years?"

Many students prepare only for year one. Explain how the rest will be paid: continuing income, savings, a scholarship that renews, or an assistantship.

!!! Important
Never use borrowed money that is placed in an account just before your interview, and never use a statement that has been edited or "arranged" by an agent. Officers are trained to spot sudden large deposits. Fake documents can lead to a permanent ban from the United States. If your finances are complicated, get them explained honestly instead.

## Questions about your plans after graduation

US law presumes that every student visa applicant intends to immigrate until they show otherwise. This is the section of US law behind most refusals, known as **214(b)**. The officer wants to see real reasons for you to return.

### "What will you do after your degree?"

Describe a realistic plan in Pakistan: the kind of company or role, the industry, or a family business you will join or expand. If you plan to use OPT work experience in the US first, you can say so honestly, but explain why your long-term future is back home.

### "Do you have relatives in the USA?"

Answer truthfully. Having relatives in the US is not a reason for refusal, but hiding them is a serious problem if the officer already knows.

### "Why should you come back to Pakistan?"

Talk about concrete ties: family responsibilities, property, a job offer or role waiting for you, a business, or the career market for your field in Pakistan. Specific and personal beats general and patriotic.

## How to answer well

- **Answer the question you were asked.** Keep each answer to one or two sentences. If the officer wants more, they will ask.
- **Speak naturally.** Practise your ideas, not exact sentences.
- **Make sure your DS-160 matches what you say.** Officers have it on screen.
- **Bring documents but do not push them.** Hand over a document only when you are asked.
- **Stay calm if you are refused.** A 214(b) refusal is not a permanent ban. You can apply again when something real has changed, such as stronger finances, a clearer plan or a better-prepared answer.

## A 10-minute practice routine

1. Write your three-sentence story: why this program, how you will pay, and what you will do after.
2. Ask a friend or family member to fire the questions above at you in random order.
3. Record yourself on your phone and listen for answers that sound memorised or too long.
4. Do one full mock interview with someone who has actually been through the F-1 interview.
